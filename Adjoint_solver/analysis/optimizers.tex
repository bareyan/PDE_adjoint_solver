\section{Goal}
We study the inverse problem of recovering a potential $V$ in a nonlinear PDE from a target state
$u_0$. The report asks when this problem is solvable. We compare several optimizers on a
well-conditioned and an ill-conditioned instance, trace the ill-conditioned failure to the Hessian of
the misfit (its conditioning and a null space in $V$), and show how regularization restores a usable
recovery.

%=====================================================================
\section{Problem setup}\label{sec:setup}
%=====================================================================
We consider the nonlinear PDE
\[
-u'' + \alpha u^3 + V u = f, \quad x \in [0, 2\pi], \qquad u(0) = u(2\pi),\quad u'(0) = u'(2\pi),
\]
where $u$ is the state, $V$ the potential to recover, $\alpha$ a nonlinearity parameter, and $f$
a source term. We recover $V$ from a target state $u_0$ by minimizing the misfit
\[
M(V) = \int_0^{2\pi} (u(V) - u_0)^2 \, dx,
\]
where $u(V)$ solves the PDE for $V$. We measure a recovery by the relative potential error
$e(V) = \|V - V_\star\| / \|V_\star\|$.

We use two instances, both with true potential $V_\star = 3 + \cos x$. The well-conditioned one
takes $u_{\mathrm{well}} \propto 2 + \sin x$ (strictly positive); the ill-conditioned one takes
$u_{\mathrm{ill}} \propto \sin x$ (sign-changing, with zeros at $x = 0$ and $x = \pi$). Each target
is normalized to $\|u_0\|_{L^2} = 1$. The data are exact: instead of a forward solve, we fix $u_0$
and back out the source from the PDE,
\[
f = -u_0'' + \alpha u_0^3 + V_\star\, u_0,
\]
so $u_0$ solves the PDE for $V_\star$ by construction. We then recover $V$ and compare it to
$V_\star$ (\cref{fig:instances}).

%=====================================================================
\section{Adjoint gradient}\label{sec:adjoint}
%=====================================================================
To run a gradient-based optimizer we need the gradient of $M(V)$. Report~1 derives it by the
adjoint method, which returns the gradient with one extra linear solve and without differentiating
$u(V)$ directly. In the experiments we compute gradients with Zygote.jl automatic differentiation
and adjoint-based rules, the most efficient and general option we found.

%=====================================================================
\section{Optimizer comparison: the identifiability gap}\label{sec:optimizers}
%=====================================================================
We compare three optimizers: gradient descent (GD), Adam, and L-BFGS. GD is the simplest. Adam adds
adaptive step sizes and momentum; it is built for machine-learning problems but usually beats GD.
L-BFGS is a quasi-Newton method that builds an inverse-Hessian approximation, which helps on
ill-conditioned problems because it adapts to the local curvature.

We run all three on both instances. First we use a fixed iteration budget and record the loss and
the potential error $e(V)$ at each iteration (\cref{fig:optimizers}).

On the well-conditioned instance the methods that move recover $V$. L-BFGS is fastest and reaches
the lowest loss (8 iterations, under a second). Adam is slower and stops short of L-BFGS, reaching a
potential error of about $2\times10^{-4}$ at a loss near $10^{-10}$. GD is the slowest and least efficient: its curves are
almost flat (\cref{fig:optimizers}). One fixed step size cannot move the stiff
and the soft modes together, so the iterate is frozen.

The ill-conditioned instance shows the gap. The loss still falls to near machine zero, but the
potential error stalls: minimizing the misfit no longer recovers $V$. The ranking even inverts.
L-BFGS reaches the lower loss (about $2\times10^{-11}$) but the worse potential
($e(V) \approx 0.095$ in \cref{tab:optimizers}); Adam settles at a higher loss (about $10^{-9}$) but
the better potential ($\approx 0.047$). For the ill-conditioned case we have problematic points: the zeros of $u_0$ at $x=0,\pi$. The gradient of our loss w.r.t.\ $V$ is a pointwise product of the state $u$ with the adjoint variable, so it stays null wherever $u$ vanishes; at convergence $u\approx u_0$, so $V$ cannot be updated there. A lower loss does not mean a better recovery. The reason is
structural, we can see in the next plot \Cref{fig:recovered} how the different optimizers shape differently the outputs:
The lbfgs optimized V has oscillations around the problematic points, while Adam is more straightforward and forms sharp spikes at those points.

\section{The misfit Hessian: conditioning and identifiability}\label{sec:hessian}

To analyze further the behaviour of optimization algorithms for this problem, we analyze the Hessian to  identify complexity of the optimization.
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Closed-form Hessian}\label{subsec:hessian}
From Report~1 we have the linearisation $L = -\partial^2 + \mathrm{diag}(3\alpha u^2 + V)$
and the sensitivity $J = -L^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(u)$. The misfit is
\[
M(V) = \frac{w}{2}\|u(V) - u_0\|^2,
\]
with residual $r = u - u_0$ and $w = 4\pi/N$, so that $M$ is the trapezoidal form of the integral in \cref{sec:setup}. By the adjoint method,
\[
\nabla M = w\,J^\top r = -w\,\mathrm{diag}(u)L^{-1}r = -w\,\mathrm{diag}(u)\,\lambda,
\]
where $\lambda = L^{-1}r$ is the adjoint state.
Differentiate $\nabla M = w\,J^\top r$ using the product rule:
\[
H\,\delta V = \dot{(\nabla M)} = w\big[\,\dot{(J^\top)}\,r + J^\top\dot r\,\big].
\]

The easy term: $\dot r = \delta u = J\,\delta V$, so
\[
J^\top\dot r = J^\top J\,\delta V \qquad\text{(Gauss--Newton term)}.
\]

The hard term uses $J^\top = -\mathrm{diag}(u)L^{-1}$. By the product rule and the inverse rule on $L^{-1}$:
\[
\dot{(J^\top)} = -\mathrm{diag}(\delta u)L^{-1} + \mathrm{diag}(u)L^{-1}\dot L\,L^{-1}.
\]
Applying this to $r$ and using $L^{-1}r = \lambda$:
\[
\dot{(J^\top)}\,r = -\mathrm{diag}(\delta u)\,\lambda + \mathrm{diag}(u)L^{-1}\dot L\,\lambda.
\]

Simplify each piece. For the first: $\mathrm{diag}(\delta u)\lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\delta u = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)J\,\delta V$.
For the second, expand $\dot L\,\lambda$ using $\dot L = \mathrm{diag}(6\alpha\,u\odot\delta u + \delta V)$:
\[
\dot L\,\lambda = 6\alpha\,\mathrm{diag}(u\odot\lambda)\,\delta u + \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\,\delta V.
\]
Then $\mathrm{diag}(u)L^{-1}\dot L\,\lambda = -J^\top[\,6\alpha\,\mathrm{diag}(u\odot\lambda)J\,\delta V + \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\,\delta V\,]$.

Combine both parts of the hard term:
\[
\dot{(J^\top)}\,r = -\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)J\,\delta V - 6\alpha\,J^\top\mathrm{diag}(u\odot\lambda)J\,\delta V - J^\top\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\,\delta V.
\]

Assemble the full Hessian by adding the Gauss--Newton term and the hard term:
\[
H\,\delta V = w\big[\,J^\top J - 6\alpha\,J^\top\mathrm{diag}(u\odot\lambda)J - J^\top\mathrm{diag}(\lambda) - \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)J\,\big]\delta V.
\]

Thus
\[
H = w\,(J^\top J + S),
\qquad
S = -6\alpha\,J^\top\mathrm{diag}(u\odot\lambda)J - J^\top\mathrm{diag}(\lambda) - \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)J.
\]
The term $J^\top J$ is the Gauss--Newton component; every term in $S$ carries $\lambda$ and thus depends on the residual $r$.
The Hessian is symmetric.


\subsection{Conditioning and identifiability}\label{subsec:conditioning}
We plot the computed Hessian in position space (\cref{fig:hessian})
and its spectrum (\cref{fig:spectrum}). The Gauss--Newton component 
whose $(i,j)$ entry is $\tfrac{4\pi}{N}\,u_i\,(L^{-2})_{ij}\,u_j$ explains the
smoothing band and the loss of identifiability at zeros of $u$

For the well-conditioned instance ($u_{\mathrm{well}} \propto 2 + \sin x > 0$) the factor
$\mathrm{diag}(u)$ is invertible, so the Gauss--Newton component has the full rank of $L^{-2}$ and
the Hessian spectrum decays
smoothly. The decay still matters: $L^{-2}$ damps high-frequency modes of $V$, so those directions
are weakly constrained, but none is lost. Every direction in $V$ is identifiable.

For the ill-conditioned instance the spectrum drops to machine zero, a null space. The cause is the
factor $u$: $u_{\mathrm{ill}} \propto \sin x$ vanishes at $x = 0$ and $x = \pi$, so the matching rows
and columns of the Gauss--Newton component are zero, the dead cross-bands in \cref{fig:H-ill}. The
same factor appears in the
gradient $-\tfrac{4\pi}{N}\,(u\odot\lambda)$, which cannot move $V$ where $u = 0$. The misfit is flat
along these directions, so the data do not constrain $V$ at the zeros of $u$, and the problem is
non-identifiable there. This is exactly the failure seen in \cref{sec:optimizers}:
\cref{fig:recovered} shows the recovered $V$ matching $V_\star$ except at $x = 0, \pi$.

\subsection{Dependence on nonlinearity and resolution}\label{subsec:dependence}
The nonlinearity $\alpha$ and the resolution $N$ change the problem without changing its kind. They
act on different parts of the full Hessian. The smoothing and rank effects are already visible in
the Gauss--Newton component so we sweep them separately.

\subsubsection{Nonlinearity $\alpha$}
$\alpha$ enters the full Hessian through $L = -\partial_{xx} + \mathrm{diag}(3\alpha u^2 + V)$ and
through the correction $S$. A larger $\alpha$ adds $3\alpha u^2$ to the diagonal of $L$, lifting its
smallest eigenvalues (the smooth, low-frequency modes, where the $-\partial_{xx}$ term is weak),
while the largest eigenvalue, set by $-\partial_{xx}$ at $O(N^2)$, is essentially unchanged. So
$\kappa(L) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ shrinks as $\lambda_{\min}$ rises, and since $H$ carries
$L^{-2}$ the conditioning of $H$ drops with it. The sweep confirms this
for the well case: $\kappa$ falls about three orders of magnitude, from $3.7\times10^{10}$ at
$\alpha = 0.5$ to $1.6\times10^{7}$ at $\alpha = 500$ (\cref{fig:alpha}, \cref{tab:alpha}), while the
Newton solve stiffens and needs more iterations. The ill case behaves differently: $\mathrm{diag}(u)$
is singular at every $\alpha$, so $\kappa$ stays near $10^{28}$ and the recovery error stays around
$0.08$ to $0.10$. Nonlinearity improves the conditioning of the well problem but does not remove the
null space of the ill one.

\subsubsection{Resolution $N$}
Resolution acts on $L^{-2}$. The eigenvalues of $L \approx -\partial_{xx}$ run from $O(1)$ for the
smoothest mode to $O(N^2)$ for the highest mode the grid resolves, so those of $L^{-2}$ run from
$O(1)$ down to $O(N^{-4})$. For the well case $\mathrm{diag}(u)$ is bounded and invertible and does
not change this, so
\[
  \kappa(H) \sim N^4 ,
\]
the straight line in \cref{fig:kappaN}. A finer mesh exposes finer, less constrained modes of $V$. The ill case
has $\kappa$ near $10^{30}$ at every $N$, set by the null space and the round-off floor, with no
clean trend.

\section{Regularization}\label{sec:regularization}
%=====================================================================
Regularization helps in three ways. It stabilizes the inversion when $u_0$ is noisy; it adds prior
knowledge about $V$, such as smoothness or sparsity; and it fills in the null directions of the
Hessian, so $V$ can be recovered where the data alone cannot constrain it.

Noise makes the need clear. We perturb $u_0$ by about $10^{-6}$, too small to see (\cref{fig:direct},
right), and try the pointwise inversion that solves the PDE for $V$ directly,
\[
  V_{\mathrm{direct}} = \frac{f + u'' - \alpha u^3}{u}.
\]
This differentiates the noisy $u$ twice, and the $u''$ term scales a Fourier mode of wavenumber $k$
by $k^2$, so the high-frequency noise is amplified and $V_{\mathrm{direct}}$ is too noisy to use
(\cref{fig:direct}, left). The variational recovery $V^\star$ never differentiates the data and
stays close to $V_\star$.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{\datadir/direct_vs_optim_well.pdf}
  \caption{Left: the pointwise inversion 
           $V_{\mathrm{direct}} = (f + u'' - \alpha u^3)/u$ and L-BFGS recovery on noisy well-conditioned data
          Right: the added noise is invisible to the naked eye.}
  \label{fig:direct}
\end{figure}

The penalty has to match the unknown. We compare two, each scaled by $\beta$ and added to the misfit:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Tikhonov $R_2(V) = \beta\,\|V'\|_2^2$: favors smoothness
  \item Total variation $R_1(V) = \beta\,\|V'\|_1$: favors piecewise-constant shapes with sharp edges
\end{enumerate}
Which one wins depends on $V_\star$. For a smooth $V_\star$ (a cosine) Tikhonov matches the prior and reaches about
$85\times$ lower error than TV at the best $\beta$ (\cref{fig:reg-smooth}, \cref{tab:regularization});
at $\beta^\star$ it also removes the null-point spikes of \cref{sec:optimizers}. 

For a box $V_\star$
(a top-hat) the order flips: TV keeps the edges that Tikhonov smooths out (\cref{fig:reg-spike}).
Regularization helps when the penalty matches the structure of the unknown, supplying what the data
leave undetermined.
